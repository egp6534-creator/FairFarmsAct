The Three Pillars · How the Bill Works

A complete restructuring built around three principles.

I.
Cap by operation, not by entity.

The "(except a joint venture or general partnership)" carve-out at 7 U.S.C. § 1308(b) is struck. Payment limits attach to the farming operation, with full attribution through unlimited ownership layers. The 2014 Farm Bill family exemption is repealed. A 1,000-hour active-engagement test applies uniformly. Restructuring as a partnership, LLC, or trust no longer multiplies the cap.

II.
Pay for stewardship, not volume.

Base acres sunset over five years. ARC and PLC are replaced with practice-based payments tied to cover cropping, rotation diversity, soil organic matter, and habitat. A diversification index rewards farms that grow more than two crops. The narrower the portfolio, the smaller the support — flipping the historical incentive structure entirely.

III.
A new track for the cities.

Title VII opens a parallel program for controlled-environment and vertical agriculture sited in food-access priority tracts. Industrial-scale capital that gets capped out of Title I commodity support has somewhere productive to go: 40% construction credits for urban facilities, federal procurement guarantees, and conditions on renewable power and living wages.

The shape of the American countryside is not destiny. It is the precipitate of the choices Congress makes about who gets a check.

— § 101, Findings & Purpose

Deep Dive · The Two Largest Distortions

Two provisions do most of the structural damage.

Distortion A · The Partnership Multiplier

Six words in 7 U.S.C. § 1308(b) erase the payment cap for any operation willing to restructure.

The statute reads: "The total amount of payments received... by a person or legal entity (except a joint venture or general partnership) for any crop year... may not exceed $125,000." That parenthetical is the entire game. A corporation, LLC, or limited partnership is itself capped. But a general partnership or joint venture is not — the cap attaches to each partner individually.

The mechanical result: a single 8,000-acre operation organized as an 8-person general partnership (operator, spouse, two adult children, sibling, two cousins, niece) collects up to $1.24 million in commodity payments where the same operation organized as a corporation would receive $155,000.

The OBBBA of 2025 made this worse by extending the multi-cap treatment to LLCs and pass-through entities. The Fair Farms Act strikes the parenthetical entirely — and applies the cap to the operation, looking through whatever legal shells sit between the cap and the ultimate beneficial owner.

Distortion B · The Family Carve-Out

The 2014 Farm Bill § 1604 simultaneously told USDA to tighten the rules and forbade them from applying the new rules to family farms.

When Congress directed USDA in 2014 to define "significant contribution of active personal management," it added a one-paragraph exemption: "The Secretary shall not apply the regulations promulgated pursuant to subsection (a) to individuals or entities comprised solely of family members."

Because the statute defines "family member" expansively — lineal ancestors and descendants, siblings, spouses, first cousins, nieces, nephews, plus everyone "by marriage" — and because 96% of U.S. farms are organized as family operations, the exemption swallows the rule. A family-member general partnership with eight adult relatives spread across three states can run an 8,000-acre operation while qualifying every member as "actively engaged" with no on-site presence required.

The Fair Farms Act repeals § 1604 in full. Family relationship has no bearing on payment eligibility. Genuinely small family farms are protected by the Tier I designation — by their actual size — not by the legal status of who they're related to.

§ 204 · Anti-Circumvention & Aggregation

Splitting an operation into legal shells to claim a lower tier is a Class B violation.

The four-tier system collapses if operators can simply restructure into multiple smaller entities. Section 204 closes that door. Operations under common management — defined as sharing equipment, labor, marketing, or land-management decisions across two or more legal entities — are aggregated as a single operation for tier purposes.

The Secretary shall promulgate rules to identify common management through equipment-sharing patterns, shared FSA records, common addresses of beneficial owners, and seven additional indicia. Splitting an operation into multiple legal shells to claim a lower tier triggers five-year program disqualification and clawback of all payments received during the violation period.

Page IV · Structure

Eight titles. One coherent system.

The bill restructures Title I commodity programs and adds new program titles built alongside. Each title can be read independently, but the bill's force comes from how they interlock.

Open any title to read its core provisions. Title II provides the eligibility framework; Title I rebuilds payment structure around stewardship outcomes; Title III adds the diversification multiplier. Titles IV through VII handle insurance, land access, regional infrastructure, and the new urban innovation track. Title VIII handles transition.

Detail

Title I is the structural heart of the new system. Where ARC and PLC tied federal payments to commodity volume and historical base acres, the Stewardship Foundation ties them to verified ecological practices on currently-farmed land. The four practice payments are designed to stack additively — a single operation can simultaneously earn cover crop, rotation diversity, soil carbon, and habitat payments.

Page V · The Transition

The math flips in year four.

Regenerative practices lower production costs over time — less synthetic fertilizer, less herbicide, less irrigation, less soil erosion to repair. But the transition years are the hard part. Yields can dip, learning curves are steep, and conventional debt structures don't bend. The Fair Farms Act builds that bridge with a dedicated transition program designed to carry farmers across the cost-curve crossover.

01
The Economic Case · Why Regenerative Pays Back

A subsidy that pays for itself by reducing the need for future subsidy.

Conventional row-crop agriculture in the U.S. has spent eighty years optimizing for one variable: yield per acre per year. That optimization comes at the cost of soil structure, organic matter, water-holding capacity, and biological function — which producers then replace, year after year, by purchasing synthetic nitrogen, phosphorus, herbicides, fungicides, and increasingly expensive irrigation.

Regenerative practices invert the equation. Cover crops fix nitrogen biologically and suppress weeds physically; diverse rotations break pest cycles without spraying; managed grazing rebuilds organic matter that holds water through droughts; reduced tillage cuts fuel and equipment costs. The catch is that these practices take three to seven years to fully replace what the soil had been doing on borrowed credit.

The Fair Farms Act treats that bridge as the highest-leverage federal investment in agriculture. Every dollar spent helping a producer transition is a dollar that doesn't need to be spent on disaster relief, on soil-erosion remediation, on water-quality cleanup, or on the next round of commodity supports when the conventional system fails again.

Why This Pays For Itself

The federal balance sheet, viewed across a decade.

The transition program is structured as a front-loaded investment that reduces ongoing federal liability. Three lines of savings accrue over the ten-year window following enactment.

Reduction A

Lower disaster aid & ad-hoc payments.

Operations with rebuilt soil organic matter and diverse rotations are dramatically more resilient to drought, flood, and pest pressure. CBO modeling of similar transition programs shows 15–25% reductions in federal disaster relief expenditure for participating regions over the decade following full transition.

Modeled savings
$2.1B – $3.4B over 10 years
Reduction B

Lower water-quality remediation costs.

Synthetic nitrogen runoff is the dominant driver of Gulf hypoxia, Chesapeake Bay degradation, and groundwater contamination across the Midwest. Reducing synthetic N application by 40% on transitioned acres produces measurable downstream savings in EPA, Army Corps, and state-level remediation budgets.

Modeled savings
$800M – $1.6B over 10 years
Reduction C

Lower commodity-program payments.

Diversified operations are less commodity-price-sensitive — they have multiple income streams that move on different cycles. Producers fully transitioned by year five draw less from ARC/PLC and marketing-loan programs in subsequent commodity downturns.

Modeled savings
$1.2B – $2.0B over 10 years
Net

A self-financing investment by year seven.

Combined modeled savings of $4.1B–$7.0B over a decade exceed the program's authorized cost of $3.8B over the same window. The Transition Subtitle is the only major program in the Fair Farms Act that scores as deficit-reducing on its own.

Net 10-yr impact
+$0.3B to +$3.2B deficit reduction

The cheapest soil to build is the soil you have. The cheapest farmer to keep in business is the one already on the land. The Fair Farms Act spends federal dollars in years one through three so it can spend fewer of them in years four through twenty.

— § 350(a), Findings of the Transition Subtitle
06
Farmer Feedback · What the Research Missed

The system doesn't reach the smallest producers at all.

Feedback from a local farm using sustainable practices revealed a gap the original design had not accounted for: their gross cash farm income falls below any threshold that would qualify them for the programs built around the Tier I classification. Per-acre stewardship payments on a small operation round down to a payment too small to affect decision-making. The two additions below address that gap directly.

§ 351 · Direct Equipment & Materials Grant

Up to $8,000 per year — farmer chooses what they need.

A flat, non-competitive grant covering the full cost of specific items a farm needs to operate. No per-acre calculation. No minimum revenue. No percentage match. If you have documented farming activity, you qualify — and you choose what gets covered based on what you don't already have.

$8,000
Per farm, per year. No GCFI floor. No acreage minimum. Any producer with documented farming activity is eligible regardless of scale. If you already have a seed-starting greenhouse, you don't apply for that — you apply for your cover crop materials instead.

How It Works

At enrollment, the producer submits a simple checklist of what they already have from the eligible items list. The grant covers the full purchase cost of selected items they don't already have, up to the $8,000 annual limit. Items can be split across multiple categories — a farm that needs irrigation and cover crop seed can apply for both. The grant renews annually, allowing farmers to build out their infrastructure one piece at a time based on evolving needs.

This design came directly from stakeholder feedback: "If they already have a greenhouse for starting seeds, they can have their material for cover crops paid for." The farmer chooses, the system pays.

$2K

Minimum Stewardship Payment — Tier I Flat Floor

Per-acre stewardship payments on a 3-acre market garden produce less than $200 per year — less than the cost of enrolling in the program. The minimum floor fixes this by guaranteeing every enrolled Tier I producer at least $2,000 per year in stewardship payments, regardless of acreage. If the per-acre calculation produces more, the higher amount applies. If it produces less, the floor kicks in. No small farm walks away with a payment that doesn't cover its own paperwork.

Title VII · The Innovation Track

A new track for the cities — and the corporations that can build there.

The Fair Farms Act takes a deliberately different approach with industrial-scale capital. Rather than letting it crowd out small operators on rural land, Title VII opens a parallel program designed to channel large-firm investment toward controlled-environment agriculture, urban food systems, and the technological frontier of farming.

Why parallel rather than additive? Title VII recipients cannot also draw Title I commodity support — the anti-substitution clause at § 707 prevents arbitrage between rural commodity payments and urban tax credits. A corporation that wants to participate in federal agriculture programs at scale chooses one track or the other.
01
Title VII · Section by Section

Seven provisions, structured to channel capital where it serves the public.

§ 701
Vertical Farm Construction Credit

A 40% federal tax credit on capital expenditure for indoor vertical farms, hydroponic, aeroponic, and aquaponic facilities sited in census tracts designated as food-access priority areas.

Cap & Eligibility$25M maximum credit per facility. Must be sited in a food-access priority tract as designated by USDA in consultation with HUD. Construction must commence within 24 months of credit certification.
§ 702
Adaptive Reuse Bonus

An additional 15% credit if the facility occupies a vacant industrial, retail, or commercial structure rather than new construction.

Qualifying StructuresBuildings vacant or substantially underutilized for 24+ months. Industrial warehouses, big-box retail, obsolete office buildings explicitly qualify. Stacks with § 701.
§ 703
Renewable Power Tie

Credits available only to facilities sourcing at least 70% of energy from on-site or contracted renewable generation in year one, scaling to 100% by year seven.

ScheduleYear 1–2: 70% renewable. Year 3–4: 80%. Year 5–6: 90%. Year 7+: 100%. Failure triggers credit recapture and program disqualification.
§ 704
Urban Producer Purchase Guarantee

Federal feeding programs commit to purchasing $400 million annually in produce from Title VII facilities for the first decade of the program.

Buyer ProgramsDOD commissaries and military feeding, VA hospital systems, federal cafeteria contracts, and BIA boarding-school nutrition programs.
§ 705
Public Research Pipeline

Recipients must publish anonymized yield, energy-use, water-use, and operating-cost data to a USDA open-data portal.

ReportingQuarterly anonymized data submission. Trade-secret protection for proprietary cultivars and software, but operating metrics are public. ARS administers the portal.
§ 706
Living Wage & Local Hire

Eligibility conditioned on paying not less than 1.4× local prevailing wage and sourcing at least 60% of workforce from within 25 miles of the facility.

ComplianceAnnual wage and hire audit by Labor in coordination with USDA. Violations trigger credit clawback and three-year disqualification from federal procurement.
§ 707
Anti-Substitution Clause

Title VII funds cannot be drawn by an entity that simultaneously holds Title I support — preventing arbitrage between rural commodity payments and urban tax credits.

AttributionApplies through full ownership chain. An LLC subsidiary of a Title I-participating corporation cannot draw Title VII credits.

03
The Conditions · Why Title VII Doesn't Become a Giveaway

The credits come with conditions designed to keep the program honest.

Recipients of Title VII credits must agree to all of the following:

  • Site eligibility. Facility must be located in a USDA-designated food-access priority census tract.
  • Renewable power escalator. 70% renewable energy in years 1–2, scaling to 100% by year 7.
  • Living wage floor. All workers paid at minimum 1.4× local prevailing wage.
  • Local hire commitment. At least 60% of workforce sourced from within 25 miles.
  • Public data contribution. Anonymized operating metrics published quarterly to USDA's CEA Open Data Portal.
  • Sole-track participation. Cannot simultaneously draw Title I commodity payments through any owned subsidiary.
  • Not eligible if: the entity, parent, or any subsidiary holds an existing ARC/PLC contract.
  • Not eligible if: the entity is using Title VII as a substitute for residential or commercial development that would otherwise occur (anti-displacement).

Title I

Short Title, Findings, and Definitions

Sec. 101

Short Title.

This Act may be cited as the "Fair Farms Act of 2026."

Sec. 102

Findings.

Congress finds the following:

  1. The largest 4 percent of United States farms, defined by gross cash farm income exceeding $1,000,000, account for 48 percent of the value of agricultural production but operate fewer than one-third of agricultural acres.
  2. The largest 10 percent of farms collect approximately two-thirds of all payments under the Agriculture Risk Coverage program, the Price Loss Coverage program, and federal crop insurance premium subsidies, while the smallest 86 percent of operations receive a residual share.
  3. Federal crop insurance premium subsidies are uncapped, are not subject to any adjusted gross income limitation, and are statutorily confidential at the recipient level under section 502(c) of the Federal Crop Insurance Act, denying the public any visibility into the largest single subsidy stream in the agricultural support system.
  4. The exemption for family-member operations at section 1604 of the Agricultural Act of 2014 forbids the Secretary of Agriculture from applying tightened active-engagement rules to operations comprised solely of family members, and because 96 percent of United States farms are organized as family operations, the exemption swallows the rule it accompanies.
  5. The general partnership and joint venture exception at section 1308(b) of title 7, United States Code, permits operations to multiply payment limitations by the number of partners, frustrating the statutory cap on its face.
  6. Median household income for operators of large-scale family farms exceeded $410,000 in 2024, well above the threshold at which a federal safety net is reasonably justified.
  7. The shape of the American countryside is not destiny. It is the precipitate of the choices Congress makes about who receives federal support.
Sec. 103

Definitions.

In this Act:

Operation
The term "operation" means a unit of agricultural production under common management, regardless of the number of legal entities through which the operation is organized, and includes any affiliated entity sharing equipment, labor, marketing, or land-management decisions with the primary operator.
Tier
The term "tier" means one of the four producer classifications established under section 202.
Stewardship Practice
The term "stewardship practice" means an agricultural practice qualifying for payment under Title III of this Act, including cover cropping, multi-crop rotation, soil organic matter improvement, and pollinator and riparian habitat establishment.
Food-Access Priority Tract
The term "food-access priority tract" means a census tract designated by the Secretary, in consultation with the Secretary of Housing and Urban Development, as having limited access to fresh produce retail and median household income below 80 percent of area median.
Beginning Producer
The term "beginning producer" means a producer who has been actively engaged in farming for not more than 10 years preceding the date of application.
Title II

Producer Tier & Eligibility

Sec. 201

Repeal of Family Member Exemption.

Section 1604(b) of the Agricultural Act of 2014 (Public Law 113-79) is hereby repealed in its entirety. The Secretary shall apply all eligibility regulations promulgated under this title uniformly, without regard to the family relationship of the participants in any farming operation.

Sec. 202

Establishment of Producer Tiers.

Section 1308 of title 7, United States Code, is amended by adding the following at the end:

"(j) Producer Tier Classification.—For purposes of administering all programs under this title and titles I and XI of the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, as amended, the Secretary shall classify each operation into one of the following four tiers:

"(1) Tier I — Beginning & Small Producers: Operations with GCFI of less than $350,000.

"(2) Tier II — Mid-Size Producers: Operations with GCFI of $350,000 to $1,000,000.

"(3) Tier III — Large Producers: Operations with GCFI of $1,000,000 to $5,000,000.

"(4) Tier IV — Industrial Producers: Operations with GCFI exceeding $5,000,000.

Sec. 203

Active Engagement; The Thousand-Hour Test.

Section 1308–1(b)(2)(A) of title 7, United States Code, is amended:

Statutory Edit · 7 U.S.C. § 1308-1(b)(2)(A) "(A) a person... shall be considered to be actively engaged in farming with respect to a farming operation if—
(i) the person makes a significant contribution (based on the total value of the farming operation) of—
(I) capital, equipment, or land, or a combination thereof; and
(II) personal labor or active personal management; personal labor or on-site management of not less than 1,000 hours per crop year, documented by payroll records, tax filings, or contemporaneous logs; remote management activity, including communications conducted from a location more than 50 miles from the principal place of the operation, shall not count toward the 1,000-hour threshold;"

The Secretary shall promulgate implementing regulations within 180 days of enactment, and the regulations shall apply uniformly to all operations regardless of the family relationship of the participants.

Sec. 204

Anti-Circumvention; Common Management Aggregation.

The Secretary shall promulgate regulations identifying common management across two or more legal entities through, at minimum, the following indicia: (1) shared equipment use; (2) shared labor or payroll; (3) shared marketing or grain storage; (4) shared land-management decision-making; (5) common addresses of beneficial owners; (6) shared FSA records; (7) overlapping principal operators; (8) shared crop insurance agents; (9) common bank accounts; (10) any other factors indicative of common management.

Operations identified as under common management shall be aggregated as a single operation for purposes of tier classification, payment limitations, and program eligibility. Wilful misrepresentation shall constitute a Class B violation, triggering five-year program disqualification and full clawback of all federal payments received.

Sec. 207

Repeal of the Partnership Multiplier.

Section 1308(b) of title 7, United States Code, is amended:

Statutory Edit · 7 U.S.C. § 1308(b) "The total amount of payments received, directly or indirectly, by a person or legal entity (except a joint venture or general partnership) for any crop year under [the relevant programs]... may not exceed $155,000 the applicable tier ceiling established under section 202."
Title III

Stewardship & Diversification

Sec. 301

Replacement of ARC and PLC.

The Agriculture Risk Coverage and Price Loss Coverage programs are replaced as the primary commodity-support architecture by the practice-based payment system established under this title. Existing contracts shall be honored through the natural term of the contract.

Sec. 302

Stewardship Practice Payments.

The Secretary shall make annual payments to eligible producers for the verified implementation of stewardship practices, calculated as follows:

(1) Cover Cropping Premium.—$35 per acre for cover crops planted on at least 60 percent of cultivated acres, increased to $55 per acre for multi-species cover crop mixes including at least one legume.

(2) Rotation Diversity Bonus.—$25 per acre for documented three-crop rotations; $50 per acre for four-or-more-crop rotations including at least one small grain or legume.

(3) Soil Carbon Performance Payment.—A per-ton payment scaling with the verified increase in soil organic matter, measured on five-year cycles by an approved third-party verifier.

(4) Pollinator and Riparian Habitat Payment.—$200 per acre for buffer strips, native plantings, and edge habitat exceeding the minimums established by the NRCS.

Sec. 303

Diversification Index.

Stewardship payments shall be increased by the following percentages:

(1) Two commodities or fewer: no bonus.

(2) Three commodities: 10 percent.

(3) Four commodities including at least one small grain: 20 percent.

(4) Five or more commodities including at least one legume and one perennial: 35 percent.

Title IV

Reformed Risk Management

Sec. 401

Premium Subsidy Reform.

Section 508(b) of the Federal Crop Insurance Act is amended such that federal premium subsidies on crop insurance shall be tier-graduated as follows:

(1) Tier I: 80 percent premium subsidy, with no producer deductible on the first $50,000 of indemnities.

(2) Tier II: 65 percent premium subsidy.

(3) Tier III: 45 percent premium subsidy.

(4) Tier IV: 35 percent premium subsidy, subject to an absolute cap of $125,000 per operation per crop year.

Sec. 403

Repeal of Recipient Confidentiality.

Section 502(c) of the Federal Crop Insurance Act is hereby repealed. The Risk Management Agency shall publish, not later than one year after the date of enactment, an annual public database of all recipients of federal premium subsidies, including the name of the recipient, the operation, the legal-entity ownership chain, and the dollar amount of subsidies received.
